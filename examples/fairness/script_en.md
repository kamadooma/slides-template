# From Fair to Fairness — the idea of fairness in the West · speaking script (15 min, plain words)

## 1. Title

Today I will talk about how the English word "fair" became the idea of "fairness", and how the fairness we talk about today was built up over time.


## 2. Intro · the word fair

First, the English word "fair" has a slightly surprising history. The Old English word fæger originally meant "beautiful" or "pleasing to look at". For weather, it meant "sunny". In the early 1200s the meaning "just" appears. By the mid-1300s we find "fair" meaning "impartial" or "unbiased". The noun "fairness" gets the meaning "impartiality" in the mid-1400s. So if we look only at the word, "fair" was not originally a technical term in ethics. A word for beauty and pleasantness came to express rightness and fairness too.


## 3. Two histories

But here we need to be careful. This word history does not prove that the idea of fairness itself was born from the idea of beauty. Ancient Greece and Rome already had rich discussions about justice. So today I will keep two histories apart. One is the history of the English word "fair". The other is the history of ideas: what we call just, and what we call fair. How did these two come together in the modern world? That is the central question today.


## 4. Aristotle

Let's go back to ancient Greece. Aristotle discusses justice in Book 5 of the Nicomachean Ethics. The important point is that he does not think of justice as simply "treating everyone the same". He separates at least two kinds of justice. One is distributive justice: by what standard should we share out things like honour and wealth? A fair share is not the same amount for everyone. The question is the standard and the ratio. The other is corrective justice: how do we repair a balance that was broken by a deal or by harm? Here we find a question that is still with us. Is fairness giving everyone the same thing? Or giving different things, according to situation, contribution and need? In today's words, the first is equality and the second is equity. They are not the same thing.


## 5. Equity · epieikeia

Aristotle also talks about an idea usually translated as "equity". Rules are written for everyone, in a general way, so that they fit many situations. But in real life, some people are exceptions. If we apply the rule to them exactly as written, the result can actually be unfair. In that case we go back to the question "what is this rule for?" and judge the case accordingly. That is equity. This looks a lot like today's AI problem. A machine applies the rule with no exceptions. So applying the same rule to everyone does not automatically mean treating everyone fairly.


## 6. Rome and the Middle Ages

Later, in Roman law, a formula for justice became important: "give each person what belongs to them". Justinian's Institutes define justice as "the constant and perpetual will to give each person their right". In the Middle Ages, Thomas Aquinas joined Aristotle's philosophy with Christian theology, and organised justice as a virtue in our relations with other people. Long before the English word "fairness" existed, people were already arguing about what a right share is and how rules should be applied. The concept of fairness is older than the use of the word in that sense. That is why the modern idea of fairness did not come from a single word or a single school of thought.


## 7. Modern age · rules of society

In the modern age, justice and fairness widen out. They are no longer only about personal virtue or decisions in court. They become a question of how to justify the rules of society. Who may own what? To whom, and how, should the law apply? On what basis should rights and duties be set? These questions had an important place in modern natural-rights theory, social-contract theory and political philosophy. The thing to remember here is that "fairness" did not pull the history of justice along by itself. Different concepts, each with its own history, converged on the modern problem of fairness: justice, equity, rights, equality and liberty.


## 8. Rawls · justice as fairness

As a key turning point in this history, I want to introduce the 20th-century political philosopher John Rawls. In his 1971 book A Theory of Justice, Rawls set out a theory called "justice as fairness". Here, fairness is not simply "treating people without bias". It is the question of whether the basic institutions and rules of society can be justified as fair to free and equal citizens. Rawls uses a thought experiment: when we think about the rules of society, we imagine that we do not know which position in society we will hold. This is the "original position". The condition that we do not know our class, talents, gender, race or social position is the "veil of ignorance". If you did not know whether you would be born into a rich family or a poor one, whether you would be in a strong position or a weak one, what rules would you choose? Rawls's aim is to stop people in a particular position from choosing rules that suit them, and to find principles of justice that everyone can accept as free and equal citizens.


## 9. Rawls's principles

In his theory, equal basic liberties come first. And where social and economic inequalities are allowed, there must be fair equality of opportunity, and the inequalities must work to the benefit of the least advantaged. The important point is that Rawls is not saying "make everyone the same". The fact that some people are rich and some are poor does not by itself make a society unfair. The question is whether you could accept the rules that create that gap, whatever position you were born into. For example, a gap where doctors earn more is acceptable if it makes healthcare better and improves the lives of the worst-off. But a gap where rich parents buy a better school is not, because you would not choose it before knowing where you would be born. So Rawls's fairness widens the problem from the fairness of individual actions to the fairness of society's institutions as a whole. Fairness is no longer the simple principle "do the same to everyone". It becomes a question in political philosophy: how do we justify the rules of society?


## 10. Impact

If I put everything so far into one sentence, it is this: applying the same rule to everyone is not the same as treating everyone fairly. This idea runs from Aristotle's equity to Rawls's fairness of institutions. And today this question appears in a new form, in the field of AI.


## 11. 21 definitions

Today this question appears in a new form in the field of AI, for example in systems that screen job applicants or score loan risk. In 2018, the computer scientist Arvind Narayanan gave a tutorial called "21 fairness definitions and their politics". He explained that machine learning has many definitions of fairness, and each one carries a different value judgement. What is happening here is not that engineers are failing to turn fairness into numbers. The point is that there are different positions on what we should call fair in the first place. Do we care most about equal outcomes between groups? About treating every individual by the same standard? About accurate predictions? Or about repairing past discrimination and disadvantage? Statistics alone cannot answer these questions. Ancient theories of justice and AI fairness are not historically the same thing. But the problem of what to distribute, what to equalise and which imbalance to repair keeps coming back in new forms.


## 12. Conclusion

Finally, let me sum up. First, "fair" widened historically from "beautiful, pleasing" to "just, unbiased", and "fairness" developed as the noun for impartiality. Second, the history of the idea of fairness is much wider than the English word. Greek theories of justice, Roman law, medieval theology, modern political philosophy and today's AI ethics each dealt with different problems, and together they built up a long debate about justice and fairness. Third, fairness today is not the same as simple equality. Applying the same rules, equalising outcomes, improving the position of the disadvantaged, and justifying institutions fairly are different demands. Sometimes they collide. So when we talk about fairness, just saying "we should be fair" is not enough.


## 13. What is modern Western here

So what is "modern Western" about this history? I think there are four things. First, the unit that receives fairness is the individual, not the family or the community. Second, fairness is treated as a question of rules: general rules that apply to everyone, and how to apply them, rather than relationships and roles. Third, the idea that fairness means treating your own people and strangers alike. A judge does not sit on a case involving their family. A teacher does not grade their own child more kindly. The veil of ignorance is the extreme form of this: forget who you are, then think. But in the Analects there is a story: if a father steals a sheep, the son hides it, and that is uprightness. Protecting your family is not unfair; it is right. The starting point is the opposite. Fourth, the reason a rule is right is "would everyone agree?". If you ask "why is this rule right?", the answer is "because free and equal people would agree to it". Not "because it has always been so", "because God decided", or "because it keeps the whole working". All four of these come to the front only in the modern age, the age of social-contract theory and Rawls. Aristotle's justice was about life in the city-state, and Aquinas's justice was a virtue in our relations with others. In ancient and medieval times, it was more about the community. So it is more accurate to say "modern Western" than simply "Western".

## 14. Maybe not universal

Seen this way, modern Western fairness may not be universal. Each of those four assumptions has an alternative somewhere. Against "the individual is the unit", there is the African idea of ubuntu, "a person is a person through other people", or Confucian thought, which starts from one's role in the family. Against "thinking in rules", there are views in which the right treatment is what fits this situation and this relationship, rather than the same rule for everyone: the ideas of li and wa. Against "impartiality", there are traditions like Confucian graded love, where putting your parents first is a virtue, not unfairness. Against "justified by agreement", there are views that ground rightness in divine law, tradition, or the harmony of the whole; Islamic ʿadl, for example, is grounded in divine law. So when we say "we should be fair", unless we decide which fairness we are talking about, we will talk past each other.

## 15. Closing

Let me end by returning to the first question: is fairness a Western concept? The English word "fairness" has its own particular history. But the problems of fairness and justice themselves cannot be limited to the West. What matters more is that we do not treat a definition formed in one language or one philosophy as the single standard that applies to every society. Fairness, I think, is not one finished definition. It is a word for keeping the question open. Thank you.
